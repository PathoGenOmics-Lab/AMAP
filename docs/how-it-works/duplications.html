Docs / How it works

Duplication metrics

AMAP looks at gene duplication twice: within each genome, and across the whole set.

How the communities are built#

Each corrected proteome is searched against itself with Diamond, excluding self-hits. A pair is kept when the aligned identical residues cover 80% or more of either the query or the subject, so a short domain match between two otherwise unrelated proteins is not counted as duplication. Surviving pairs become edges in a graph, and connected components become duplication families.

All samples are then pooled and searched all-against-all. The resulting graph is partitioned into homologous communities with the Louvain algorithm, and the metrics below are computed per community. Results land in DUPLICATIONS/homologous_communities.tsv, with an interactive network in the matching HTML file.

The metrics#

Each appears in the table suffixed 1 and 2, for the two levels of the analysis.

  • Degree centrality (AverageDegree1-2): Average number of direct connections per node. High values mean genes in the community are similar to many others in it, the signature of a large expanded family.
  • Closeness centrality (AverageCloseness1-2): Average distance from a node to every other. High closeness means a compact community where members are all mutually similar, rather than a chain of progressively diverging copies.
  • Betweenness centrality (AverageBetweenness1-2): How often a node sits on the shortest path between two others. A gene with high betweenness bridges otherwise separate subgroups, often an intermediate form linking two diverged subfamilies.
  • Eigenvector centrality (Eigen1-2): Weights a node's connections by how well connected its neighbours are. Identifies the genes at the core of a family rather than at its edges.
  • Katz centrality (Katz1-2): Like eigenvector centrality but counting distant nodes too, with weight decaying by distance. More stable on sparse graphs where eigenvector centrality concentrates on one dense cluster.
  • Network density (Density1-2): Realised connections as a fraction of possible ones. Near 1 means every gene resembles every other, an unresolved family of near-identical copies. Low density means a sparse, diverged group.
  • Clustering coefficient (ClusteringCoefficient1-2): How often two neighbours of a node are themselves connected. High values mean well-defined subgroups.
  • Average path length (AveragePathLength1-2): Mean shortest-path distance over all pairs. Short paths mean any member is reachable from any other in a few steps.
  • Transitivity (transitivity1-2): The clustering coefficient applied to the whole network: the overall tendency to form triangles.
  • Modularity (modularity1-2): How cleanly the network divides into communities. High modularity means dense groups with few links between them, so the partition into families is well supported. Low modularity means the split is somewhat arbitrary.

Reading them together#

Two patterns come up repeatedly.

Dense, high-modularity communities are recent expansions: many near-identical copies, clearly separated from everything else. In MTBC these are typically PE/PPE genes and IS elements.

Sparse, low-modularity communities are ancient duplications that have diverged, or, less happily, an artefact of assembly fragmentation where one gene broken across contigs looks like several partial copies. Cross-checking against the contig count in *-AMAP.qc.tsv distinguishes the two.

Duplications and assembly quality

Repetitive families are exactly what short-read assemblers collapse or fragment. A genome with markedly fewer duplications than its relatives may have collapsed them rather than lost them. The metrics describe the assembly you provided, not necessarily the organism.

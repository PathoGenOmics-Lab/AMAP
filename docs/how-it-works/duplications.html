Docs / How it works

Duplication metrics

AMAP looks at gene duplication twice: within each genome, and across the whole set.

How the families are found#

Each corrected proteome is searched against itself with Diamond, excluding self-hits. A pair is kept when the identical residues of the alignment cover 80% or more of either the query or the subject, so a short domain match between two otherwise unrelated proteins is not counted as duplication. Kept pairs become links in a graph, and its connected pieces become the duplication families, listed in DUPLICATIONS/{sample}/duplications_{sample}_groups.tsv.

Because the rule takes the larger of the two shares, a fragment joins the family of the gene it came from: a match that covers almost all of a short protein counts, however little of the long one it covers.

How the communities are built#

The proteins of every sample's families are then pooled and searched all against all. Within each genome, the pairs that pass the same rule make a network, which the Louvain method splits into communities, with a fixed seed so that a rerun splits it the same way. Two communities are homologous when at least one hit joins them, and each homologous pair is one row of DUPLICATIONS/homologous_communities.tsv, with an interactive network in the matching HTML file.

The columns#

A row describes two communities: the columns ending in 1 are the first, those ending in 2 the second. For each:

  • Genome, Community: the sample, and the community's number in it.
  • Size: how many proteins it holds.
  • Elements: the eggNOG COG categories of its proteins, with how many have each.
  • AvgDegree: links per protein, on average. In a community of n proteins it reaches n - 1 when every protein is linked to every other.
  • Density: the links it has as a share of the pairs it could have. 1 means every protein matches every other.
  • AvgClustering: for each protein, how often two of its partners are linked to each other as well, averaged over the community. 1 when every protein is linked to every other, 0 for a chain or a star.
  • AvgPathLength: how many links separate two of its proteins, on average, over its largest connected piece.
  • Modularity: computed with the whole community as a single group, which makes it 0 every time. It does not tell communities apart.
  • Nodes: the proteins themselves.

Reading them together#

Density and clustering at 1 mean copies that all match each other: a recent expansion of near-identical copies. In CDC1551 these are the transposases of insertion sequences, the ESAT-6-like Esx proteins and PE proteins.

Lower density and longer paths mean copies that have drifted apart, each still matching some of the others but not all, as M. bovis's copies of the transposases above have. Or, less happily, an artefact of assembly fragmentation, where one gene broken across contigs looks like several partial copies. Cross-checking against the contig count in *-AMAP.qc.tsv tells the two apart.

Duplications and assembly quality

Repetitive families are exactly what short-read assemblers collapse or fragment. A genome with markedly fewer duplications than its relatives may have collapsed them rather than lost them. The metrics describe the assembly you provided, not necessarily the organism.

The correction algorithm

Every rule and threshold, drawn on real genes.

14 min read

The core of AMAP. It referees between the initial Bakta annotation and the miniprot alignments of a trusted reference proteome, classifying every candidate gene and then applying a set of rules to resolve the disagreements.

Coordinates throughout are 1-based inclusive, so a gene's length is end - start + 1. Every comparison is strand-aware and contig-aware: genes on opposite strands at the same position are different genes and never conflict, and neither do genes on different contigs of a draft assembly, where every contig starts at 1. Bakta runs with --keep-contig-headers so that it names the contigs as the FASTA and miniprot do; by default it renames them contig_N.

The steps in order:

  1. Placements: small proteins found exactly, the pieces of cut genes placed, and a protein's placements on its paralogs' loci dropped.
  2. Classification: every Bakta call sorted into a case, and the reference genes Bakta missed.
  3. Filtering case 1: a call with nothing in the reference stays only if Bakta is not known to invent it.
  4. Resolving case 4: the reference's coordinates win, unless Bakta's call is much longer.
  5. Deduplication on insert: a gene displaces one already accepted only when it is much longer.
  6. Rescuing missing genes: a reference gene no gene accounts for goes back in.
  7. Final deduplication: one place per gene, plus real copies and the pieces of cut genes.
  8. Stop codon check: every gene translated, then extended, flagged as a pseudogene or marked partial.

0. Placements

#

Small proteins

#

miniprot needs a seed of several residues and enough score to report an alignment, which a protein of a few dozen residues rarely gives it: annotating E. coli K-12 with its own proteome, it placed none of 33 proteins of 8 to 29 residues, and it put the toxin IbsC (b4665), 19 residues three of which differ from its neighbour IbsD's, on that neighbour only. Every protein without an exact, complete placement is looked for in the six-frame translation of the genome, preceded by a start codon and followed by a stop, and each exact copy found where miniprot did not already place the protein becomes a complete placement. Proteins shorter than 8 residues are not searched.

A protein of up to 50 residues with no exact copy is looked for again, allowing substitutions in at most a fifth of its residues after the first, which varies with the start codon; a stop codon in place of a residue counts as one, and the gene is then flagged as a pseudogene in step 7. E. coli O157:H7 Sakai carries ten of K-12's micro-proteins with one to four residues changed, and none was annotated. A copy with an insertion or a deletion is not found this way.

Pieces of cut genes

#

A gene a contig end cuts is placed in pieces. A draft assembly cuts the genes its contig breaks run through, and miniprot reports only the placements within 5% of a protein's best, so it places the larger piece of such a gene and not the smaller one at the end of another contig: in CDC1551 cut into 40 contigs, none of the 25 smaller pieces was annotated.

A placement was cut by its contig when all of these hold:

  • it lacks residues at one end of its protein
  • it ends within 30 bp of the contig's edge on that side
  • those residues would not fit in the bases left

What it lacks, if 8 residues or more, is aligned again as a protein of its own, and its best placement is the piece at the other contig end. This pass runs with -L 10 -n 2, so that miniprot indexes and chains the short reading frames an edge leaves. A placement of that protein which runs off a contig edge on the side of the cut, within 30 bp, is a piece of the gene and becomes a placement of the gene's protein. In the CDC1551 draft that places 22 of the 25 smaller pieces; the other three are five or six codons long.

Paralog placements

#

miniprot places a reference protein everywhere it scores within 5% of its best, so a protein with a paralog is placed at both loci. Before anything is classified, each placement is checked against where the protein belongs.

The reference proteome carries each protein's coordinates in its header (Rv1150_1278269-1278820), but those are reference coordinates, and any indel upstream shifts every position after it. So each placement's reference position is predicted from the nearest proteins placed only once, which follow the sample's local offset from the reference, and the placement closest to where the protein belongs is its syntenic one. A locus that several proteins are placed at, exactly, predicts nothing: it is a repeat the reference has in several copies and the sample in one, like the single IS6110 of M. bovis, and the reference positions of its proteins are spread over the whole genome. A placement is then dropped when all of these hold:

  • another protein's syntenic placement covers at least 50% of it, on the same strand and in the same reading frame
  • that protein matches there at least as well
  • the two proteins live in different places in the reference, so they are not simply neighbours that overlap

What this removes is a protein sitting on someone else's locus: Rv1945 on Rv1148c, the mce3 genes M. bovis has lost (RD7) on their mce1 paralogs, or the sixteen other IS6110 names on each IS6110 copy. What it leaves alone is a copy at a locus no protein claims, such as an IS6110 insertion at a site the reference lacks or a duplication in the sample, and genes that genuinely overlap, which sit in different frames (Rv1041c and Rv1042c).

When two proteins both look syntenic at one locus, which happens when a deletion removes a gene and leaves its paralog next door as the nearest place left, the better match keeps the locus. CDC1551 lacks Rv3427c and Rv3428c, and without this Rv3425 and Rv3426 would sit on Rv3429.

1. Classification

#

Every Bakta CDS is compared against the miniprot alignments.

Case 2, perfect match. Same start, same end, same strand as an alignment, and the same length. Correct, kept as is.

Case 3, imperfect match. Same coordinates, but the aligned length differs, typically from indels. Reliable enough, kept.

Case 4, conflicting overlap. A Bakta CDS and an alignment overlap on the same strand, but their coordinates do not match. This is the conflict that has to be adjudicated.

Case 1, potentially novel. A Bakta CDS with no overlap to any alignment. Either a genuine gene absent from the reference, or something Bakta invented.

Missing reference genes. An alignment with no overlapping Bakta CDS. The reference has a gene there and the annotator missed it. Alignments with no aligned bases at all are discarded first.

Each set is written to its own table under CDS_CASES/, so the whole decision is auditable.

2. Filtering case 1

#

Novel-gene candidates are scrutinised before being believed. A candidate is dropped if either holds:

  • it overlaps the false-positive set by 80% or more of its own length, meaning it is a call the annotator is known to make wrongly on the reference genome itself
  • it aligns to the reference at 80% or more match identity, meaning it is not novel at all but a shifted version of a gene that is already there

Candidates that pass both, and candidates with no reference alignment to judge by, are kept as case1_kept.

3. Resolving case 4

#

Single overlaps

#

One Bakta CDS against one reference alignment, on the same strand of the same contig. Lengths are compared, and:

  • the reference wins by default
  • the Bakta version wins only if it is longer by more than 550 bp, and the alignment is not complete in the call's reading frame

The asymmetry is deliberate. A reference alignment is usually the better boundary, but a much longer Bakta call normally means the alignment fragmented, and discarding a full-length gene in favour of a partial alignment would be the worse error. Beyond length:

  • A complete alignment has not fragmented. It covers the whole reference protein down to its stop codon, so a longer call in its frame ends at the same stop and only starts further upstream (plcD, Rv1755c, 843 bp in H37Rv and 1,545 bp in Bakta's call on CDC1551).
  • A call in another frame is another gene, and keeps its chance to win on length; the alignment then comes back in step 5.
  • A call that wins in the alignment's frame is the protein's gene, longer than in the reference, and takes its name in step 5.
  • A Bakta CDS that overlaps more than one alignment loses: the reference wins regardless.

Multiple overlaps

#

Several Bakta CDS against one alignment, the signature of a gene that has been split. The best-overlapping Bakta CDS is picked first, then compared to the alignment on length:

  • the reference wins if it is at least as long, if the Bakta CDS beats it by less than 500 bp, if the alignment is complete in the call's frame, or if the call overlaps other alignments as well, which makes it several reference genes read as one (Rv3022c and Rv3021c are one reading frame in CDC1551)
  • otherwise the Bakta CDS wins

4. Deduplication on insert

#

Each winner is checked against the genes already accepted. Where a new coordinate overlaps an existing one by 70% or more on the same strand:

  • the new gene displaces the existing one if it is longer by more than 550 bp
  • it also displaces it if the existing gene is shorter by less than that margin and is not a reference gene
  • otherwise the existing gene stands and the new coordinate is dropped

A reference gene is one whose identifier is a reference protein's. The test used to look for coordinates embedded in the identifier, which are stripped as the alignments are read, so no gene qualified and a call of similar length could displace any of them: Bakta's version of Rv1614, one codon longer, replaced it.

5. Rescuing missing genes

#

A reference protein that no gene accounts for goes back in, at its best placement. A gene accounts for a placement when it sits on it:

  • on the same contig and strand
  • in the same reading frame, since genes that really overlap sit in different frames (Rv0724A and Rv0725c)
  • covering at least 50% of it
  • and not known to be another reference gene, since some are nested in the same frame (E. coli hokC inside mokC)

Placements on other genes' loci have already been dropped in step 0, so what is left to rescue sits where the gene belongs or at a locus nobody claims.

A longer call takes the name of the protein it beat. A gene that stands for no reference protein accounts for any placement in its frame: the Bakta call that won its locus in step 3 over a shorter alignment. When no gene carries the protein's name, that call takes it, as long as the alignment spans at least half of the protein; without this the gene came out with no name and the protein was missing from the table. Two calls named this way:

  • in M. bovis, H37Rv's mmpL6 (Rv1557) is the 3' end of a 2,904 bp gene that the TbD1 deletion cut short in H37Rv
  • CDC1551 and M. bovis carry a 4,821 bp DNA methyltransferase gene whose first 506 residues are H37Rv's Rv2024c

A remnant names nothing: the RD12 deletion of M. bovis leaves the last 67 of cyp141's (Rv3121) 400 residues at the end of a 321-codon gene that is not cyp141.

The pieces of a cut gene are parts of its protein, not copies of it, so each is missing on its own, and one in place does not keep another out. In the CDC1551 draft a 7.5 kb Bakta call displaced the larger piece of Rv0100, which comes back here though the smaller piece is in.

6. Final deduplication

#

Where one gene ends up at several coordinates, entries fully contained inside a different gene are dropped, always keeping at least one entry per gene so nothing disappears entirely.

After the stop codon check, one gene per reference protein comes first, and the rest stay only as real copies or pieces. deduplicate_rvs.py ranks the genes that share a reference protein:

  1. a non-pseudogene over a pseudogene
  2. then the longer one
  3. then one Bakta also called at exactly those coordinates
  4. then the one nearer where the protein belongs by synteny

The first keeps its place. The others stay as further copies of the gene when all of these hold, and anything else is a remnant of the gene and goes:

  • at least 90% as long as the first
  • clear of the copies kept
  • not covered by another reference gene
  • for a pseudogene, also 90% as long as the reference protein, which a piece of a split gene is not

A genome can carry a gene more than once, and these copies are what the duplication analysis counts: E. coli O157:H7 Sakai keeps 34 that used to be deleted.

The pieces of a cut gene stay as well, when the first gene is one of them and all of these hold:

  • each is marked partial at its cut end
  • no two are open at the same end alone: a gene has one first piece and one last, and any number between that a contig holds whole
  • they are clear of each other
  • together they are at most 110% of the reference gene's length

Copies and pieces only miniprot placed are all named after their protein, so later ones get _2, _3 to keep identifiers unique.

7. Stop codon check

#

check_cds.py translates every surviving CDS.

It is given the reference proteome, which settles what a translation alone cannot.

No terminal stop codon. Coordinates are extended to the next in-frame stop, searching 300 bp in the right direction for the strand, or as far as the reference protein says is missing when that is further. If the extension introduces internal stops or finds nothing, the original coordinates are kept and the gene is flagged as a pseudogene.

An extension goes no further than the reference protein. A gene may be extended to a stop at most 10 residues past the end of its reference protein: that completes an alignment that stopped short. A frame that runs on further has lost its stop or shifted, and the C-terminus it would add is not the gene's; the gene keeps its extent and is flagged as a pseudogene, with the reason, as PGAP and Bakta treat a frameshift. Often that frame runs on into the next gene, whose stop is then the next one (in M. bovis Rv0590 into Rv0590A and Rv3373 into Rv3374), and extending would annotate that gene twice.

Genes a contig cut short are partial, not pseudogenes, and each piece of a cut gene is marked this way, under the gene's name:

  • 3' partial: in a draft assembly, a gene whose frame reaches the end of its contig with no stop, from within 30 bp of it, was cut by the assembly, and runs to the last codon there.
  • 5' partial: a gene that opens within a codon of the contig's edge without a start codon, or with a codon that can start a gene but whose protein begins inside its reference protein. A cut leaves whatever codon it falls before at the edge, and one in six codons inside H37Rv genes is one a gene can start with.

Reference pseudogenes keep their extent. When the reference protein spans its annotated stretch codon for codon with no stop (PE21, Rv2099c; PPE48, Rv3022c) and the gene ends where it does, the reference annotates it without a stop, and the gene is left as it is and flagged. A reference pseudogene annotated with a partial final codon (oxyR', Rv2427A) gets that base back, since an alignment only covers whole codons.

Clipped N-termini are restored. When a gene ends in a stop but is short of its reference protein at the start, and the codons upstream open with a start codon and translate to the missing residues, the gene is extended to cover them. A gene that already starts where its reference does is shorter somewhere inside, and is left alone.

Internal stop codons. Flagged as a pseudogene and kept, with the flag propagated to the master table and the GFF3. *-AMAP.clean.faa excludes them.

Untranslatable genes are dropped from the annotation table as well as the FASTA files, so the two never disagree.
